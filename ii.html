Theory 1 · Counterpoint

b d p q: one motif, four forms

Bach and Schoenberg varied a melody by playing it backwards (retrograde), upside down (inversion), or both. b, d, p and q are drawn exactly that way, so here they play one three-note motif in its four forms. Tap one to hear it.

Theory 2 · Musical cryptograms

Names that hide melodies

In German, notes are named A to H, and S sounds like Es (E flat). Composers used this to spell names as music. Switch on Note names and only those letters sound; the rest go silent.

Theory 3 · The basics inside the machine

Music theory already at work

Scale

Everything plays in A natural minor, the Aeolian mode. Numbers are scale steps: 1 A, 2 B … 8 A again.

Octave

An octave up vibrates twice as fast. Tall letters go up one; tail letters go down one.

Overtones

Smooth waves have few overtones and sound pure. Corners add many and sound bright. Each card below shows its first eight.

Dynamics

Bigger shapes play louder, smaller shapes softer.

Articulation

Narrow letters are staccato, wide ones legato. Doubles tie, vowels glide, spaces rest.

Phasing

Text that is not 16 steps long drifts against the beat, like in Steve Reich's phase music.

Theory 4 · Drawn sound

The Alphabeat's ancestors

Turning drawings into sound has a history. The Alphabeat adds the alphabet to it.

1950s–70s

Norman McLaren drew soundtracks straight onto film, as in Synchromy (1971).

1960s

Daphne Oram built Oramics, a machine that played shapes painted on film strips.

1977

Iannis Xenakis created UPIC, where composers drew waves on a tablet and heard them.

Now

The Alphabeat: every letter is a drawn wave, so writing becomes composing.
